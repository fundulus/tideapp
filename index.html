About the data

United States stations

Predictions come live from the NOAA CO-OPS API, covering all US coasts plus Alaska, Hawaii, the territories and the Great Lakes. Heights are relative to MLLW (mean lower low water). These are NOAA's own published predictions, not a model of them.

Many smaller sites are subordinate stations: NOAA predicts them from a nearby reference station and publishes only their highs and lows, with no continuous curve. For those the times and heights shown are still NOAA's exactly, but the curve drawn between them is this app's, a smooth interpolation that passes through every published turning point and arrives flat at each.

Mexican stations

These four sites work differently, and it is worth knowing how. Bahía de los Ángeles, Bahía de San Quintín, Ensenada and Loreto are not served by any free prediction API. Their tides here are computed from harmonic constants derived in-house: CICESE (predmar.cicese.mx) publishes monthly tide calendars, and two years of those published tables (2025 and 2026) were parsed and fitted by least squares to recover the underlying harmonic constituents.

The fit uses Schureman equilibrium arguments with nodal f and u corrections applied, so the constants are properly referenced and stay valid outside the years fitted, rather than absorbing one year's lunar node position. Each published high and low also contributes a zero-derivative constraint, which pins the model's turning points to the published ones.

Accuracy. Fitting on 2025 alone and predicting 2026 blind, against CICESE's own published values:

Heights are relative to BMI (bajamar media inferior), which is equivalent to MLLW, so the two sources are directly comparable. Because the constants are embedded in this page, these four sites need no network connection at all.

Clock caveat. CICESE prints each monthly calendar on a single time meridian: standard time for November through March, daylight time for April through October. Real daylight saving in Baja California's border municipalities starts mid-March and ends in early November, so for roughly three weeks each March a printed CICESE table sits an hour behind civil time. This app shows civil time, and flags those dates when you view them.

How much to trust a station

Not all NOAA stations are equally well measured, and the difference is large enough to matter. A badge appears beside the station number when there is a caveat worth knowing; a station with nothing to declare shows nothing.

Short record. Two tidal constituents can only be told apart if the record is long enough for them to drift a full cycle out of step. K1 from P1, and S2 from K2, each need 182.6 days. Analysed from anything shorter, they cannot be measured and have to be inferred from equilibrium theory instead. That is detectable: inference leaves both amplitude ratios within a fraction of a percent of the textbook values, where genuinely measured ones sit several percent off, because real coastlines are not textbooks. This app tests the published constants themselves rather than trusting any metadata, and needs no network to do it.

It matters because P1 modulates K1 through the year and K2 modulates S2 over six months, so inferring them produces timing error that drifts with the season and the spring-neap cycle rather than showing as a constant offset. Mission Bay (Campland) is the local example: three months of data from February to April 2017, with the seasonal terms borrowed from La Jolla.

How big is that error in practice? It cannot be checked against real water, because stations analysed from a short record were usually temporary deployments and most, Campland included, no longer report observed levels. It can still be bounded, though, because NOAA happens to publish two independent predictions for Mission Bay: Campland, from its own three-month analysis, and Quivira Basin, derived from San Diego. Over 2026 those two disagree by a median of 4 minutes, 11 minutes at the 90th percentile, and up to 35 minutes.

For comparison, Quivira Basin is tied to San Diego by fixed offsets and so never departs from it by more than 5 minutes. The extra scatter is Campland's short-record analysis drifting, exactly as the inference test predicts. So roughly ten minutes, occasionally worse, is a fair expectation for a short-record station, and it is not a constant offset you could correct for: it moves with the season and the spring-neap cycle.

None of this is a gap between this app and NOAA. On a connection you are seeing NOAA's published predictions verbatim, and offline this app recomputes them from NOAA's own constants to within about a centimetre and a minute or two. The uncertainty is shared, because the constants are shared.

Subordinate station. Not measured in its own right at all. NOAA predicts it from a nearby reference station by shifting each high and low in time and scaling it in height, and publishes no continuous curve.

Working without a connection

Any station can be kept offline. Open it and press keep offline beside the station number. The app pulls down NOAA's published harmonic constants for that station, stores them on the device, and from then on it computes there with no connection at all, for any date. Useful when you already know the site you are heading to has no signal. Press it again to remove it. Subordinate stations work too: their reference station is fetched and kept alongside them automatically.

The four Mexican sites need no network at all, and five San Diego sites are carried in the page the same way, so they keep working out of signal: Mission Bay (Campland), Quivira Basin (Mission Bay), La Jolla, Imperial Beach and San Diego (Broadway Pier). Nothing is fitted for these: they use NOAA's own published harmonic constants, and the two that are not harmonic stations are built the way NOAA builds them, from San Diego's tide shifted in time and scaled in height by NOAA's published offsets.

Sun and moon

Sunrise and sunset are computed from each station's latitude and longitude and aligned to its local clock. Moon phases use the Meeus algorithm, which agrees with CICESE's published phase times to under a minute.

Limitations

Everything here is a tide prediction. Real water levels depart from prediction with weather, notably barometric pressure, wind and river discharge, by amounts that can exceed the errors quoted above. Not for navigation.
